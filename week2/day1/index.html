Week 2 · Day 1 · Notes

A model that grades models

Last week, the reward came from code that checked a number. This week, the reward comes from a judge model. It's flexible and fast, but it has blind spots your policy can learn to exploit.

1

Why judges, and how well they work

Summaries, explanations, advice and writing have no single right answer. People can grade them but are slow and costly, and RL needs millions of grades. A strong model grades in seconds and explains its reasons.

In the MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena study (Zheng et al., 2023), strong judges like GPT-4 agreed with human preferences over 80% of the time, about the same rate at which humans agree with each other. The same paper documented three biases: position, verbosity and self-enhancement.

FormatQuestion to the judgeGoodWatch out
Pointwise"Score this answer 1–10."Simple; one call per answerScores bunch at 7–8 and drift between calls
Pairwise"Which of A and B is better?"More consistent; how classic RLHF collected dataPosition bias; needs a second answer to compare to
Reference-guided"Does this cover what the reference covers?"Concrete and stableNeeds a good reference per prompt
3

Rubrics: turn "good" into checks

Good rubric items are specific ("mentions the Friday deadline"), observable (you can point to the sentence) and independent (each item checks one thing). Weight the items that matter most.

Item (task: summarize an email for a busy manager)WeightExample result
States the main request3✓
Mentions the deadline2✓
Under 80 words1✗
No invented facts3✓
Reward = passed weight ÷ total weight8 / 9 = 0.89

Rubrics as rewards is an active research direction: "Rubrics as Rewards" (Gunjal et al., 2025) trains with GRPO using checklist-style rubrics graded by a judge, and reports that rubrics help smaller judges most. Day 5 goes deeper.

4

The three biases, and the fix for each

BiasWhat happensFix
PositionIn pairwise grading, the judge favours the first (or second) answerAsk twice with the order swapped; keep a winner only if both orders agree, else tie
VerbosityLonger answers look more thorough. Under RL, the model learns to padLength limits in the rubric, compare similar lengths, always plot answer length
Self-enhancementThe judge prefers answers that sound like its own model familyUse a judge from a different family; check it against human labels (Day 2)
5

Plumbing: prompt, parse, cost

You are grading a response.

[TASK]
{task}

[RESPONSE]
{response}

[RUBRIC]
1. States the main request (weight 3)
2. Mentions the deadline (weight 2)

Think step by step about each rubric item. Then end with one line exactly like this:
SCORE: <number from 1 to 10>
  • Parse strictly. Take the last SCORE: n that ends a line. Out of range or missing → None, and log it. Never fall back to a default high score: that rewards confusing the judge.
  • Cost. 64 prompts × 8 answers = 512 judge calls per GRPO step. Training judges are small and fast, served with an inference engine, with caching. Big judges are for evaluation.

Glossary

LLM judge
A language model that grades another model's answer.
Rubric
A checklist of what a good answer should do, often with weights.
Pointwise
Score one answer on its own, e.g. 7 out of 10.
Pairwise
Show two answers and ask which is better.
Reference answer
A known good answer the judge compares against.
Position bias
Preferring the first (or second) answer shown, regardless of quality.
Verbosity bias
Preferring longer answers even when they add nothing.
Self-enhancement bias
Preferring answers that sound like the judge's own model family.
Parsing
Reading the score out of the judge's free text.
Agreement
How often the judge picks the same as people do (Day 2).
Rubrics as Rewards (RaR)
Using checklist rubrics graded by a judge as the RL reward.
Caching
Storing judge results so repeated answers aren't graded twice.

Build the judge's plumbing

Four small functions you'll reuse all week, plus a rubric for a task you know. Send me both.

Tasks

  1. Write judge/judge_utils.py with:
    • build_judge_prompt(task, response, rubric, lo=1, hi=10): rubric is a list of (criterion, weight); items appear as 1. criterion (weight w); ends with SCORE: <number from lo to hi>; ValueError on an empty rubric
    • parse_score(text, lo=1, hi=10): the last SCORE: n (or n/10) that ends a line, as a float; None if missing or out of range
    • rubric_reward(checks): checks is a list of (weight, passed); returns passed weight ÷ total; ValueError if empty or any weight ≤ 0
    • pairwise_debiased(first_order, swapped_order): verdicts are "first", "second" or "tie"; returns "X", "Y" or "tie"
